1. Introduction

Some mathematical ideas are powerful because they allow us to ignore details that are not essential to the question. Topology is one such language: instead of measuring exact distances, it asks which properties remain unchanged under continuous deformation.

The purpose of this note is to introduce the basic viewpoint of topology and to illustrate how definitions, propositions, and proofs can be presented in a mathematical article.

2. A definition to begin with

Definition 2.1 · Topological space A topological space is a pair \((X, \mathcal{T})\), where \(X\) is a set and \(\mathcal{T}\) is a collection of subsets of \(X\) such that:
  1. \(\varnothing, X \in \mathcal{T}\);
  2. arbitrary unions of elements of \(\mathcal{T}\) belong to \(\mathcal{T}\);
  3. finite intersections of elements of \(\mathcal{T}\) belong to \(\mathcal{T}\).
The elements of \(\mathcal{T}\) are called open sets.

The definition is compact, but it opens the door to a broad family of objects and questions. The point is not merely to generalise distance; it is to identify which structures are worth preserving.

3. A theorem-shaped perspective

Theorem 3.1 · Illustrative statement A continuous image of a compact topological space is compact.
Proof. Let \(f:X\to Y\) be continuous and let \(X\) be compact. Given an open cover of \(f(X)\), its inverse images under \(f\) form an open cover of \(X\). Compactness gives a finite subcover, whose corresponding sets cover \(f(X)\). Hence \(f(X)\) is compact. ∎

This example illustrates the standard structure of a mathematical argument: state the result precisely, identify the relevant hypothesis, and use the definitions to establish the conclusion.
